Freelancers live and die by their portfolio. But a portfolio alone isn't a website. What turns visitors into clients is a combination of clarity, proof, and an easy next step. Here's what a freelancer's site should include.

## A clear statement of what you do

One sentence at the top. "I design brand identities for small food and beverage companies." "I write conversion copy for B2B SaaS landing pages." Specificity wins more work than versatility does.

## A portfolio of your best work

Not everything you've ever made — just your best. Six to ten strong pieces, each with a short case study: the brief, what you did, and the result. Quality beats quantity every time.

## Your services

List what you offer and how you work: project-based, retainer, hourly, or packaged. Ambiguity about scope and pricing costs freelancers more inquiries than anything else.

## Testimonials

Specific quotes from named clients (with permission) do more than a wall of logos. One strong testimonial beats ten vague ones.

## Your process

What does it look like to work with you? Discovery call, proposal, deposit, kickoff, delivery? A short walkthrough reduces friction and helps clients say yes faster.

## Availability

Are you open for new projects? Booking for next month? Fully booked? Letting visitors know your current status saves everyone time.

## A simple way to reach you

A contact form, a scheduling link, or a direct email. Freelancers who make it easy to start a conversation win more work.

A freelancer's website is often the difference between being perceived as "someone who does this" and "someone I want to hire." Small details — clarity, proof, ease — make that difference.
