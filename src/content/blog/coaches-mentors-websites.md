Coaches and mentors sell transformation. But transformation is hard to communicate in a social post. A website is where you explain your method, show your results, and give people a clear way to work with you.

Here's what to include.

## Your niche

Life coach, business coach, executive mentor, career coach — the more specific, the better. "I help mid-career professionals transition into tech" is far stronger than "I help people reach their potential."

## Your method

Coaching is often intangible. Naming your framework — even a simple three or four-step process — makes it concrete and easy to say yes to.

## Your ideal client

Who do you work best with? Naming them attracts the right inquiries and quietly filters out the rest. This one section saves hours of mismatched discovery calls.

## Results and testimonials

Show outcomes. "After six months, she landed a role at [company]" is more powerful than "She felt more confident." Numbers, titles, and specific changes land harder than adjectives.

## Your bio

A short story, not a résumé. Why you do this work, what qualifies you, and what you believe about coaching. People buy from people, not from credentials alone.

## How to work with you

One-on-one, group programs, intensives, courses? Lay out your offerings clearly and link each one to a way to learn more or book.

## A simple booking path

A discovery call booking link is the standard for coaches. Make it obvious and easy to use — one click, no forms in the way.

Coaching websites work best when they're warm, clear, and confident. Skip the corporate speak. Write like you'd talk to a client in session.
