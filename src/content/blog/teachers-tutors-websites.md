Teaching is one of the few professions where almost everyone needs what you offer — and almost no one knows how to find you. A website solves that problem. Here's what a teacher's or tutor's site should include.

## Subjects and levels

Be specific. "Math tutor" is vague. "High school algebra and geometry tutor, grades 9–11" tells parents instantly whether you're the right fit. The narrower your focus, the more qualified your inquiries become.

## Your teaching approach

Parents want to know how you work. Do you focus on homework help, exam prep, concept mastery, or confidence building? Do you teach in person, online, or both? A short section answers these questions before anyone has to ask.

## Format and availability

In-person or online. One-on-one or small group. Weekday evenings or weekend mornings. Being clear about when and how you teach saves you from dozens of mismatched inquiries.

## Pricing or pricing structure

You don't have to list exact rates, but you should indicate whether you charge per session, per package, or monthly. Ambiguity about price turns serious parents away.

## Testimonials

Even a handful of short quotes from parents or students goes a long way. Tutoring is a referral-driven business — testimonials are the next best thing to a personal recommendation.

## A simple way to book

A contact form, a phone number, or a booking link. Parents are busy. The easier it is to reach you, the more likely they will.

Teachers and tutors rarely need a fancy website. They need a clear one that answers the questions parents are already asking.
